This Lesson is a set of eighteen practice questions for the part of behavioral interviewing that is about how you work with people, handle uncertainty and lead without a title. The questions are grouped into six themes. Each theme has a short explanation, three questions to practise aloud, and a note under every question on what strong evidence in an answer tends to look like. The title borrows Googleyness, an informal word that appears on Google-authored public pages and is not defined here.

> *InterviewNotes-created practice material; not affiliated with or endorsed by Amazon or Google. Company hiring practices can change; these questions and signals are editorial preparation aids, not official questions or scoring rubrics.*

**What the six themes are.** They are InterviewNotes' own grouping of ideas that recur in behavioral preparation for software engineers. They are not presented as a list that Google uses, and their order carries no ranking or weighting. Nothing below describes how any company runs or decides an interview, and none of the questions is claimed to be one a company asks.

**Where the ideas come from.** Two Google-authored public pages were read as research and inspiration. A 2017 post, [summer interns answer questions about their internships](https://blog.google/company-news/inside-google/life-at-google/real-internship/), has interns describing Googleyness in their own informal words, with remarks about teamwork and helping others. A 2022 profile, [a Google Cloud engineering leader's career story](https://blog.google/company-news/inside-google/life-at-google/how-a-career-in-cloud-technology-led-johnson-to-google/), mentions inclusion and respect in leadership and, separately, advises candidates to be curious and to ask for clarification. Both are personal accounts from individuals, written years apart. Neither sets out a framework.

**How to use it.** Choose a real story before you read the note under a question, say the answer out loud, then compare. The same experience can often answer questions from more than one theme, but telling it identically each time starts to sound rehearsed, so pick the angle the question is asking about. Use only things that happened. Coursework, open-source contributions, volunteer projects and personal projects all work when other people depended on, reviewed or used what you made. If you cannot answer a question truthfully, treat that as a gap in your preparation and look for a nearby experience, not as a reason to embellish one. A familiar shape for an answer is situation, task, action and result; the notes below concern the substance that goes into each part.

## Collaboration, respect, and inclusion

Engineering is shared work, and many of its failures happen between people: a review that turns into a contest, a design shaped only by the loudest voice, a handoff that nobody owns. Questions in this theme look at how you treat the people you depend on when you do not fully agree with them, and whether the way you work leaves room for contributions you would not have thought of yourself. Specifics carry more weight than warmth: who said what, what you changed because of it, and how the other person would describe the exchange.

### A better idea from someone else {#googleyness-collaboration-respect-inclusion-01}

The question: "Describe a moment when a colleague's suggestion made your approach better. What did you do to draw it out, and what changed as a result?"

*Strong evidence tends to look like:* a specific suggestion, named plainly, followed by a visible change to the design or the code, with credit going to the person who offered it. Weaker versions describe open-mindedness in general terms, or choose a case where the colleague only confirmed what you had already planned.

### Disagreeing across disciplines {#googleyness-collaboration-respect-inclusion-02}

The question: "Tell me about a disagreement with people from another team or discipline, such as design, data or operations. How did you find a way forward without damaging the working relationship?"

*Strong evidence tends to look like:* the other side's position stated fairly, in terms they would recognise, along with what each party gave up and how the relationship stood afterwards. An account in which you were simply proven right says little about collaboration.

### Making room for more voices {#googleyness-collaboration-respect-inclusion-03}

The question: "Give an example of changing how a meeting, review or planning process worked so that people who spoke less could still shape the outcome."

*Strong evidence tends to look like:* a concrete mechanism, such as written pre-reads, rotating facilitation or collecting comments before discussion, and a sign that it worked, for example a concern raised that would otherwise have gone unspoken. Frame it as a practice that would continue without you, not as a rescue.

## Navigating ambiguity and asking good questions

Requests rarely arrive complete. A goal is stated as a symptom, a deadline comes with no definition of done, or a system has never been described end to end. This theme is about what you do in the first days. The habits involved are ordinary and visible: separating what is known from what is assumed, asking the question whose answer would change the plan, running a cheap test before committing, and telling stakeholders what is still unknown so they can disagree early. These habits carry over to system design interviews, so practice in one place can help in the other.

### The vague starting brief {#googleyness-ambiguity-clarification-01}

The question: "Think of a project that began with a goal nobody had written down clearly. What did you ask first, and why those questions?"

*Strong evidence tends to look like:* an order to the questions and a reason for it. One question chosen because its answer would change what you built is stronger evidence than a long list asked in no particular sequence.

### Requirements that kept moving {#googleyness-ambiguity-clarification-02}

The question: "Describe a project where the requirements changed more than once. How did you keep making progress without building yourself into a corner?"

*Strong evidence tends to look like:* decisions deliberately kept reversible, such as narrow interfaces, a feature flag, or postponing the costly choice, and a clear account of what you chose not to build yet. Evidence of designing for change is stronger than evidence of resisting it.

### From a request to checkpoints {#googleyness-ambiguity-clarification-03}

The question: "Tell me about a loosely worded request that you turned into a plan with clear checkpoints. How did you decide what to measure at each one?"

*Strong evidence tends to look like:* checkpoints with an observable signal attached, and an account of what you would have done if one failed. "We reviewed progress weekly" names a meeting, not a checkpoint.

## Learning and intellectual humility

Software changes faster than any one person's knowledge, so how you handle being wrong or new tells an interviewer a good deal. Intellectual humility is not self-criticism. It means holding conclusions in proportion to the evidence, saying "I don't know yet" without drama, and changing your mind openly when the facts change. Learning stories convince when they show a method: what you did to find out, how you checked your understanding, and how you passed on what you learned.

### A confident assumption that failed {#googleyness-learning-intellectual-humility-01}

The question: "Describe a technical or process assumption you were sure about and later found to be wrong. How did you discover it, and how did you respond?"

*Strong evidence tends to look like:* the moment of discovery, what or who surfaced it, and what it cost. Good answers explain why the belief seemed reasonable at the time, not treat it as obviously foolish in hindsight, and describe a change in practice and not just in one conclusion.

### Getting up to speed {#googleyness-learning-intellectual-humility-02}

The question: "Describe how you became useful quickly in a codebase, system or problem domain that was unfamiliar to you. How did you learn, and how did you know you understood enough?"

*Strong evidence tends to look like:* a method, for instance reading a system's failures before its design, pairing with someone who knew it, or making small first changes that were reviewed closely, plus a test of understanding such as a change that reviewers accepted. A list of resources you consulted is thin evidence by itself.

### Asking early {#googleyness-learning-intellectual-humility-03}

The question: "Describe a case where you asked a question, or asked for help, sooner than felt comfortable. What did it save or improve?"

*Strong evidence tends to look like:* the judgement behind the timing, meaning what you tried first, what made you stop, and how you framed the question so it was easy to answer. The result should be concrete, such as a day saved or a wrong direction avoided. Avoid cases where asking was plainly the only option.

## Adaptability and resilience

Plans fail for ordinary reasons: a dependency changes, a launch slips, a priority is replaced, or an approach that worked at small scale stops working. Questions here ask how you behave afterwards. In this setting resilience is less about toughness than about returning to useful work with an honest account of what happened, a revised plan and your working relationships intact. Adaptability is the related skill of dropping a method you are fond of once the situation no longer supports it.

### A project that fell short {#googleyness-adaptability-resilience-01}

The question: "Tell me about a project of yours that failed or fell badly behind. What did you do in the days after you knew?"

*Strong evidence tends to look like:* the first actions after the realisation, such as telling the people affected, re-scoping, and salvaging what was usable. Own your share without taking on blame that was not yours and without sliding into excuses. A failure that cost nothing is unconvincing.

### Letting go of a familiar approach {#googleyness-adaptability-resilience-02}

The question: "Describe a time a new constraint forced you to give up a method you were comfortable with. How did you adjust?"

*Strong evidence tends to look like:* what the old approach was good at, what the constraint made impossible, and how you chose the replacement. Real friction, such as being slower at first or making an early mistake, is more believable than a seamless switch.

### A setback that improved the process {#googleyness-adaptability-resilience-03}

The question: "Give an example of a setback that led you to change how your team works, beyond fixing your own part. What changed, and how did you know it helped?"

*Strong evidence tends to look like:* a durable change, such as a checklist, a test, a review step or an earlier handoff, and some signal that it worked. Be cautious about presenting a process change made after one incident without saying what evidence would show it was worth keeping.

## Leadership without formal authority

Most engineers lead before they manage: they bring a group to a decision, organise a messy effort or close a gap nobody was assigned. Without authority, influence comes from clarity, credibility and follow-through, because nobody has to do what you ask. These questions suit any level, and the best stories are usually modest in size and exact in detail: how you learned what each person cared about, what you asked of them, and how you kept the effort moving.

### Aligning people you could not direct {#googleyness-leadership-without-authority-01}

The question: "Tell me about a time you needed several people to move in the same direction and none of them reported to you. How did you get there?"

*Strong evidence tends to look like:* an understanding of what each person wanted, a specific request that was easy to agree to, and a considered response to the person who hesitated. Reaching for rank, even indirectly through a manager, is the opposite of this evidence.

### Filling a gap nobody owned {#googleyness-leadership-without-authority-02}

The question: "Describe something that needed doing, had no owner, and that you took on. How did you involve others, and how did it end or get handed on?"

*Strong evidence tends to look like:* how you noticed the gap, why you were a sensible person to act, and what remained afterwards, such as a document, a rota or a named owner. Check that the story is not only about extra work done alone; leadership shows in how others became able to continue.

### Turning discussion into a decision {#googleyness-leadership-without-authority-03}

The question: "Give an example of helping a group that kept circling a choice settle it and commit. What happened with the people who preferred a different option?"

*Strong evidence tends to look like:* the options laid out fairly, a clear point of decision or a named decider, and a written outcome with next steps. The second half of the question matters: strong answers show that the dissenting view was heard and that those people still moved on.

## Responsible impact, integrity, and judgment

Engineers regularly make choices whose consequences land on people who are not in the room: users, support teams, whoever maintains the code next. This theme covers how you weigh those effects against speed, and how you behave when honesty is inconvenient. The evidence is judgement under pressure. It shows in raising a risk before it becomes an incident, taking the slower route for a stated reason, and reporting your own mistakes plainly. Keep stories factual and proportionate. Dramatic ethical dilemmas are rare and not required.

### A risk others brushed aside {#googleyness-responsible-impact-integrity-01}

The question: "Describe a time you raised a concern that your teammates did not initially take seriously. How did you make your case, and what happened?"

*Strong evidence tends to look like:* something concrete behind the concern, such as a reproduction, a data point or a likely user impact, a constructive tone, and an honest outcome, including cases where you were overruled or turned out to be partly wrong.

### The quick path and the responsible one {#googleyness-responsible-impact-integrity-02}

The question: "Describe a decision where the quickest route would have been a poor choice for users or colleagues. What did you do instead, and what did it cost?"

*Strong evidence tends to look like:* a real cost, named: a later date, extra work or an awkward conversation. If the responsible path cost nothing, the story shows little judgement. Where others were affected, explain how you involved the appropriate people and made the consequences visible, rather than treating your own judgment as sufficient by itself.

### Being honest about a mistake {#googleyness-responsible-impact-integrity-03}

The question: "Give an example of telling others about a mistake, or about something you did not know, when staying quiet would have been easier. How did you handle it?"

*Strong evidence tends to look like:* a prompt, factual disclosure covering what happened, who was affected and what you were doing about it, ideally with a change that made a repeat less likely. Polished non-mistakes, such as working too hard, do not qualify.
